It is well worth resting for a moment on the Iron Road to drink in the sights of Jyoto. This city is a true and living testament to its founders; the Minimoto clan, the Bear clan. Function over style, strength over finesse, work over leisure, straight talking truth over pomp and decorum, for as you’re like to hear quite often in these parts “The Bear does not dance”. Jyoto is the last stop or the first on the Iron Road, depending on your perspective, a road that forms the very heart of the city, as it grows down the mountain from the Great Mine. Roads branch off in a uniquely linear fashion from this central trunk making the whole city look much akin to a giant conifer so widespread in these parts. The Iron Road passes out of the city through enormous gates set in towering thick stone walls and to continue the analogy, it acts as a root through which most sustenance passes into the city.  
 
From your position on the road, before you head in and provided clouds aren’t obscuring the mountains, you will see the Great Brown Bear, the Mon of the Minimoto, literally looking down on his people and the fine city of Jyoto. You see his visage, scars and all, is struck into the very rock, and is somewhat beautiful in its own way, a piece of art adorning a city that has turned its back on such frivolities; as the inhabitants see it, for you will come to learn if you spend any time here at all “The Bear does not dance”.  
 
The Great Brown Bear carving sits above the city proper, marking the entrance to the Great Mine. Further up the mountain still is the Shrine of Ancients and is of great importance to the Minimoto and the men of the north. It is only accessible via a winding mountain path through angry rock, barely wide enough to be traversed by one man at a time, especially when you consider the size of an average Minimoto. Pilgrims are welcome to try the inhospitable trek to the top, but there is no guarantee of either making it or being granted access if you do. Many have tried and failed to reach the Shrine, succumbing to exposure or misplaced footing leading to a slip into the sharp walls.  
 
The dedication and risk involved with this pilgrimage is considered a sign of the strength of the Minimoto’s faith. The Shrine houses the Oracle of the North, who is a conduit to the clan’s ancestors. Twice a year during the cycles of the Stubborn Earth and that of Rebirth the Minimoto leader and his first born son make the journey to consult the Oracle and use that information to guide the clan.  
 
There is such a pilgrimage story, widely remembered and revered in these parts, about Minimoto Hiroki’s (The leader of the Minimoto clan) great, great grandfather; Tiroko. An excellent leader who made huge strides for the clan, in his own time he became something of a living legend, for a time defying the Empire and the Prefecture’s taxes. Eventually in the face of the combined might of the other major Jwarian clans and a force from the mainland he backed down, but even in the face of such numbers he maintained the strength of the clan.  
 
The tale begins with one such pilgrimage during the cycle of Rebirth, Tiroko and his son Tarou made the trek to the Shrine of Ancients without incident, an easier climb than in the winter cycles. Tiroko was fiercely proud of Tarou, already bigger than most Jwarians and only twelve. A beast of a boy both lithe and strong, as skilled with a Tetsubo as one could hope, showing finesse with the weapon most thought was reserved for the Katana. Tarou respected his father without question and found great strength in his silence whilst taking any opportunity to show his age questioning and quizzing his father on the intricacies of leadership. When consulting the Oracle, flanked by her guard of Yuki no Maio, Tarou stood by quietly as his father traded the usual pleasantries, short by the standards of the other clans. And then something that took Tarou by surprise, the old lady’s body was wracked with violent shakes, before her body almost doubled up, back arched dangerously close to breaking. Her eyes now facing backwards radiated a white glow on her rocky throne, casting sharp jagged shadows on the shrine walls.  
 
Tarou was captivated by these until the Oracle spoke, a deep guttural voice that he was unable to distinguish whether it emanated from the crone before him or the very heart of the mountain. His mind raced to take meaning from the web of words weaved but nevertheless he found himself snared by confusion. Shortly after the voice ceased his father gasped, the first time Tarou had seen his father lack composure, Tiroko’s brow creasing into a considering frown. As they turned to leave, the Oracle now slumped in her chair admonished Tiroko for being so hasty, perhaps the only person in the world able to do so, and with this issued a warning; his path would lead to great triumph for the clan but also great pain as well, Tiroko not known for wasting words considered as silently as his son before issuing a low grunt accompanied by a slight nod of the head. While they were descending back to Jyoto, Tarou broke the reverie, suddenly gasping as his father had before him, as the prophecy blossomed into true understanding. This drew a hearty laugh from Tiroko, followed by a grunt and nod of shared understanding, this time accompanied by a ruffle of the boy’s hair. As they neared Jyoto, Tiroko turned to Tarou and said ‘Not a word of this, my son’.  
 
In the following cycles Tarou watched his father put the prophecy into motion, withholding taxes and causing the Shiho, the leaders of the Prefecture, to call upon the other clans to march to war. In a blatant move against the Empire he also reinstated the right of any man to rid himself of a child born during the cycle of the Bleeding Moon, an act the Emperor if true to his ancestors could not ignore. Both acts eventually led to an army camped outside Jyoto. And now the Bear, Tiroko, surprised everyone, enemy and ally alike; he agreed to parley.  
 
During negotiations in the great hall the Bear managed to turn the Snake, Eagle, Dragon, Carp and Horse aside as well as the Emperor’s envoy. They wrangled and wrestled for weeks over a spartan table of water and plain rice, the windows wide open to the icy mountain winds, the great fireplace empty, only cold bleak stone. The generals faring slightly better than the diplomats of the clans, unaccustomed to such treatment, sat across from Tiroko and his young son both in tauntingly thin woolen vests oblivious to the bitter conditions around them. The other clans’ members no doubt wishing they had a Minimoto beard to help keep the chills at bay. You must realise at this point that until now the Bear had not really negotiated in any form and any discussion of this sort that lasted longer than a day was likely to lead to blood at the table and then consequently the battlefield. As the days rolled into weeks the north grew colder and the opposing clans grew impatient, the ashigaru who were hastily assembled were eager to get home to prepare for the oncoming winter. No one had prepared for anything other than being met by a suicidal frontal assault by the brute strength of the Bear. When the first flakes of snow flowered on the mountainside, the clans realised their situation grew ever more tenuous and the time came closer that peaceful resolution would not be possible.  
 
To the surprise of all Tiroko also changed tack, seeming to anticipate how close to breaking point his guests had become. The envoys were met with a room bright and warm, a roaring fire for a moment appreciated by all for the raw beauty it possesses, their usual disdain for the lack of carving in the surrounding fireplace or other baubles or trinkets forgotten. Simple yet wholesome roasted meats were served to men, who had been reduced to field rations for too long and sake flowed freely. Tiroko matched each man in the room two drinks to one, settling their fears of a ruse. But in fairness he towered above each and every one, and could always drink well as the story is told. They talked through the day and long into the night, he had found an ally in the emperor’s envoy who was pleased by his host’s willingness to accede to all of the Emperor’s demands and he in essence turned his superior skills of diplomacy subtly in the Minimoto’s favour. By the time they were done every one of them went to bed feeling like they had achieved a victory.  
 
The Bear had backed down and would re-join the Prefecture and pay taxes again, the Minimoto would however no longer hold the title of Stewards of the West, to which the Shiho clan, the Eagle, had demanded. Tiroko had offered no more than a wry smile at this but inside his fury had burned. The most remarkable outcome of all though was a reduction in the taxes demanded of the Minimoto! A truly terrific result for the Minimoto made all the less likely by being achieved without bloodshed, it appears this particular Bear did indeed dance, and with the best of them. Whilst Tiroko was indeed pained by his loss of stewardship of the west he felt the trade-off was more than acceptable and looked forward to the next pilgrimage and his opportunity to worship at the feet of his ancestors.  
 
Again, the pair set off, father and son, alas this time neither returned. As they had almost finished climbing the steep ascent Tarou had an awful premonition, a passing glance down at the sea of jagged cliffs below brought the memory of the shadows on the Shrine wall rushing back. Absorbed in the moment Tarou, usually so sure footed, slipped. Careening over the edge his hands found little purchase whilst being ripped to shreds, buying just enough time for his father to reach out and take his hand, greasy with blood Tiroko could not hold on, before the boy slipped his final words would haunt his father forever. In a painfully resigned voice he said ‘It is fine father, it was foretold, do you not see?’  
 
Sad, it would have been interesting to see where Tarou’s leadership would have taken the Minimoto but it was not to be and so we have a world in which Minimoto Hiroki, the ‘Old Bear’ rules the clan. Interestingly his actions in life have proved him quite a pacifist unlike his brother the ‘Black Bear’ so named for leaving the clan, directly disobeying his leader, to pursue death and glory, with the mercenary group known as the ‘Thousand Moons’.  
 
Minimoto Hashimora the heir to the clan is apparently more in the ilk of his uncle than father, however he holds to the way of family and abides by his father’s rule. The people love Hashimora for all that his father is not and they call him the ‘Great Bear’.  
 
Enough reminiscing on what might have been, let us be on then and through the giant gates of Jyoto. A word of warning though, the welcome you are likely to receive will be as austere as the unadorned feel of Jyoto. As you walk the streets here the first thing you will notice is that the people are generally larger than most Jwarians. The men sport beards to fend off the cold and contrary to popular belief the women do not, alas I guess you will not believe me until you see for yourself. The appearance of the people of Jyota is a source of amusement for most other Jwarians. The joke being that if the average Jyoto citizen is so large due to years of inbreeding with the men of the mountain then to attain their size the Minimoto clan must have been having their way with the bears of which they share such affinity.  
 
The people here are mainly occupied with metal work of which there is none finer or hunting in the mountains to supplement the grains and rice that they find a necessity to import. These jobs I believe help forge their hostility to us ‘weak’ foreigners. Farming is not in their nature, they believe their mastery of metal and their physical prowess are the true teachings of Bushido and that clans that pursue other arts are merely distracting themselves and polluting the key tenets.  
 
The paradox of the Minimoto is that for a clan that has little or no interest in ornamentation they have a huge wealth of rare metals and precious gems in their treasuries. These they do however value if solely for the inconceivable value others place upon them. If the disapproving eyes of the locals get too much then a visit to a gem house for an auction may restore ones fervour to continue exploring if only because you will be surrounded by merchants. To the man they are here in the hope of turning a tidy profit, indeed if one works the auction rooms of Jyoto well, who knows where life could lead them. It is worth being aware that the Silvermoon Trade Syndicate no doubt learnt a couple of tricks from these houses, the sake flows freely here as well, no doubt driving up the price of the jewels on offer, I shouldn’t imagine.  
 
If you have your will refreshed or you are just drunk then continue up the Iron Road to the Grand Mine. As you go it is important to notice that the usual divide in districts based upon one’s occupation such as that seen most clearly in Ryu does not happen here. Yes, as you ascend the status of the occupants increases. Most minor clans that serve the Minimoto are to be found closer to the top of Jyoto, with the Mininmoto clan’s family members adorning the top of the tree, so to speak. This though I believe is due to the value placed on a man’s strength. If a merchant could swing a tetsubo as well as a samurai he would be welcome anywhere in Jyoto.  
 
is further reason than social observation to make the climb though, by the Great Mine you will find the Jewel Gallery of the Minimoto. This is perhaps the only flamboyant element to be found here in Jyoto, at odds with the rest of the city. The Jewel Gallery houses the very best of all the precious stones mined in the Iron Mountains, their beauty unparalleled. Diamonds, rubies, emeralds and sapphires have been worked by the master craftsmen of the city to adorn everything from katanas that would make a daimyo envious to jewellery fit for ladies of the Imperial Court. The clan do not keep these hidden away though and for a healthy fee in moons one can marvel at the largest selection of jaw dropping stones perhaps in the world and the supreme craft of Jyoto artisans. Those for sale in the gem houses further down the city are mere baubles in comparison to the smallest on offer here. I have pondered as to why this array of beauty is on offer and wonder if it is merely a strange show of strength; flaunting the beauty the Minimoto possess that others covet yet in which they have so little interest. Naively I once voiced this to a guard of the Jewel Gallery, I was promptly and roughly escorted out and gruffly assured “The bear does not dance”.
