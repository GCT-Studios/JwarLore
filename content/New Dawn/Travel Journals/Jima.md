Seagulls circle overhead, looking down, as you stand here amongst your fellow travellers, each jostling to get to the prow and the view it affords. Night or day the sights and sounds are there to be enjoyed. Jima maintains a carnival atmosphere at any time of year, firecrackers, musicians and dancers compete to capture audiences, all the while creating a chaotic cacophony. On an evening approach elaborate and costly fireworks illuminate the world around Jima, and surely more colourful lanterns hang here than anywhere else, so it is well worth leaving social etiquette in the cabin to ensure your view. If you don’t make it, take a moment to look out port or starboard, what is incredible is the sheer number of vessels, packed with visitors from across the Jwar Isles. This city, you see, is only approachable from the east by navigating the ‘Gulf of the Three Brothers’. A vast mountain range ‘Koun no Yama’ flanks its north and east side wrapping around it like a bear defending its cubs. Wild and dense forest, home to the last few fiercely independent Buto tribes, along Jima’s southern border make travelling arduous to the point of impossible. This bottleneck doesn’t however account for all of the people that flock to Jima, like the birds that follow in our wake, these people hold out in the hope of gaining something for nothing from Jima.  
 
Jima was not always like this, it was once merely another sleepy harbour town cradled in its own little bay, where the seas are calm and pleasant even during the cycle of the Great Wave, surrounded by mountain, forest and sea. But now it has cast aside its humble origins as it sprawls outwards. The docks; the very heart of this city constantly pump new guests through the streets of Jima, like blood running through one’s arteries, depositing their moons before returning, drained, home again. You see Jima attracts the type of person hoping to change their destiny, the man who wants everything, yesterday. This creates an almost palpable sense of excitement on the approach to Jima, quite unlike anywhere else I have had the pleasure of visiting.  
 
As your boat moors, take a moment, allow your eyes to drink in the glorious surroundings, no mere fishermen’s jetty here. Disembarking takes you immediately under cover of ornately carved pagodas, the elements will not be allowed to ruin a trip to Jima. Here is an interesting point to note, entry to the city proper is only permitted on proof of a return ticket off of the Island. I will now explain why. Jima is a gambler’s Mecca, its wealth comes predominantly from the tables of its five Luck Houses, various arenas with a wide variety of “sports” to bet on, the delightful Flower Houses and the Auction Houses of the city’s enormous trading hall. There are also those that claim it has a secondary income from an additional port somewhere on the north coast, where goods of a more disreputable nature are unloaded and then brought via secret passages, known only to a few in the upper echelons of the Silvermoon Trade Syndicate, through ‘Koun no Yama’. My readership will know however that I am not one to peddle in idle gossip.  
 
The Silvermoon Trade Syndicate mentioned are the rulers of this fine city and it is this organisation to which Jima owes its success and the rest of us owe the creation of the moon; copper, silver, gold, crescent, half or full we are all now beginning to see them as almost commonplace.  
The moon that has spread rapidly across the Empire, shoving to one side the tiresome system of bartering, was undeniably born here in Jima. Five Oyabun sit at the organisation’s head, each representing their respective gumi and each running their own Luck House and district, the boundaries of which seemingly blur at times, according to what people say. What is obvious is that any differences between the gumi is never allowed to effect the rule of the Syndicate as a whole, and besides, they are well aware if one gets rich they all do, united we stand, divided we fall, if you will. Furthermore each gumi has a specialised role within the city and each gumi relies upon and profits from the success of the others. All the gumi for instance call upon the gumi of the ‘Iron Fist’ to provide Buto trained in the martial form of Jumo for both their arenas and personal retinues of bodyguards. Similarly no gumi could maintain such excellent profits without the support of the ‘Jade Rose’. This almost entirely female led gumi maintain the Flower Houses as well as a strong trade in whispers. It is, I am led to believe, uncanny how dim-witted even the most powerful warriors or conniving diplomats can become at the hands of a Rose during the pillow talk associated with this gumi’s establishments. The identity of the leader, the Oyabun, of this secretive gumi remains a mystery, all of the Roses wear veils and who takes the mantle of ‘Queen of Flowers’ is one whisper that remains a violently guarded secret. It is rumoured, out of the hearing of any linked to the Silvermoon Trade Syndicate, that any who look upon her true face will know unparalleled beauty swiftly followed by the sweet embrace of death. Whether true or not enough tales abound to be sure this Rose most certainly has thorns!  
 
For a complete picture of the gumi that compose the Syndicate there are also the ‘Golden Dawn’ responsible for managing gambling across Jima. The ‘Three Moons’ provide favours to those who can afford them and lend moons to those that can’t, and from what I hear it is surprising just where these trails of moons lead. A cautionary note to any inspired to find their fortune in Jima, with as much gravitas as I can impart on the written page; Do not cross the Three Moons. Finally the gumi of the ‘Blue Gulls’ who run the shipping lanes in and out of Jima as well as its commercial interests at home and abroad. It is the Blue Gulls that keep the trading hall alive with artefacts, food and animals both weird and at the same time wonderful gathered from across the Empire.  
 
I have probably spent more time than I should on the organisation of the city, but it is truly fascinating to take a moment to plot the progress this city state has made in a world such as ours, establishing itself as a major power, amongst the elite and hereditary rule of the clans, in the Jwar Isles in such a short time.  
 
Once you have passed the border checks at the docks, I would suggest allowing yourself to be caught in the flow of the crowd and as if by the tide swept along towards the nearest Luck House. Depending on the fickle fate of chance you may only have the moons to enter one Luck House and I would suggest making it the central of the five, that of the gumi of the Golden Dawn. Whilst, as I have said, all the gumi run one of their own Luck Houses, this is what the Golden Dawn do best and theirs, unarguably, is the grandest of them all. It also has the magnificent trade hall of Jima at its back. I would normally suggest finding appropriate accommodation, but alas I know nothing that will stop a first time visitor changing their first port of call. There are many entrances to the House of the Golden Dawn, all the same, all containing a small cobbled courtyard. Spectacular paintings adorn the external walls depicting different scenes, my favourite being that of the Laughing Buddhas. They sit joyfully served by beautiful geisha, their happy and playful nature are a great precursor to what awaits you. Each entrance is flanked by the impressive and reassuring presence of Buto, only the most successful in the Jumo arenas are entrusted to the doors of the Luck Houses.  
 
On the other side is a splendid sight, the great hall of the Golden Dawn runs back as far as the eye can see. The circular hall runs in concentric circles, each inner circle ups the stakes, the center of all of this accessible by invitation only. Many great lords have tested the truth of this and found their family mon means little in the great halls of the Syndicate. Table after table of players, delight, sorrow, the highs and the lows all playing out their part in a kaleidoscope of emotions all under the same magnificent roof. Calling it a mere roof seems inappropriate, for the domed ceilings of the Luck Houses are truly a sight to behold. How it remains in the air without crashing down upon our heads is impressive enough but the masterpieces painted up there in the heavens are breathtaking, scenes fit for a Kami or the divine Emperor himself. I will for once abstain from trying to paint this particular picture with my words and simply request you trust my judgement that this alone is worth a visit to Jima. Whatever your game you will find it here, card, tile or luck, everyone’s favourites. The hustle and bustle is quite fantastic, the cheers of success and groans of failures all mingled with the constant jingle of moons and clinking cups overfilled with the strongest sake. Those that stay long enough will come to understand why the Buddhas on the murals look so happy, as they leave with considerably lighter purses and lighter headed than when they entered.  
 
I for one have always been blessed with the ability to happily risk a couple of copper crescent moons and come away having enjoyed the experience win or lose, a positive disposition I believe. Besides the moons I lose couldn’t buy me anywhere near as much sake anywhere else in the world!  
 
Be sure to visit the central trade hall while you are here, row after row of stalls. Each trader pronouncing his wares are the best in Jima or from the furthest afield or some other unfounded boast. The number of animals on offer for sale here is spectacular and marks out this particular market as different from all the rest I have been to. The Islands of Kiri and Kiza are the source of this wide variety. If you come along during an auction you will be able to see the arena’s great animal trainers amassed here, all vying to outbid one another for the next big thing in the arenas’ animal fighting pits. It appears to me that many simply bid purely because their fellows have, in the fear of missing out, but this could just be my cynical and untrained eye. I prefer to see the beauty in a ferocious beast as opposed to its killing potential; the caged secretary birds, prized for their ability to dispatch snakes, are very impressive, their plumage makes them look quite majestic. Many have come to the great trading hall to begin a career in animal training and quite a profit can be made from owning winning stock.  
 
The great trade hall has three floors of traders each smaller than the last, but with each ascendancy the value and quality of their wares increase. By the third you are likely to encounter priceless treasures that were once the family heirloom of some distinguished clan or other, no doubt traded with the gumi of the Three Moons to pay off the gambling debt of an errant son. The fourth and final floor of this impressive building is the headquarters of the Silvermoon Trade Syndicate, where the five Oyabun sit in council. This floor is as you can imagine off limits to the general public and if you were impressed with the standard of Buto in the Luck Houses you haven’t seen anything yet.  
 
Saving enough moons for the rest of the experiences this unique city has to offer is truly a must, but be warned the beautiful face of Jima, the ornate docks, trouble free Luck Houses and eclectic trading hall hide a darker side. As you move away from the glitz of the harbour area the number of Flower Houses and Jumo arenas decrease and the quality of the properties wane. If you still wish to venture further though be sure not to bypass the arenas completely, take in a Jumo match between two of these finely honed Buto warriors. You will understand somewhat that these giants offer far more to their roles as bodyguards and peacekeepers than just their intimidating size and disposition.  
 
Being part of the vociferous crowd as they roar on their favourite Buto or more likely the one they have backed with their own moons is like being part of your own battle. I presume. These arenas also become less prestigious as you move further from the harbour, Buto trained in the art of Jumo making way for fighting pits with a whole host of animals tearing and shredding at each other. These I find distasteful and would forego a visit, although it must be noted that for some this is the main reason they visit Jima; to delight in the unknown outcome of a match between exotic animals. It has been said the arenas once had a cat as large as a man with razor sharp teeth and claws and a head of hair that ringed its face making its appearance as noble as the Daimyo of a major clan. Its roar could apparently be heard across Jima, what a fearsome beast that must have been, and I must admit one that I would have loved to have seen and heard.  
 
As you make your way beyond the last of the arenas the city truly takes a turn for the worse. Danger, poverty and misery lurk around each corner and unless you have a need to venture to a place such as this I would recommend you didn’t. It is rumoured that even master ninja could learn a thing about moving stealthily from the back alley thieves and cutthroats that call this area of Jima home. If you took the time to consider what happens to those who rack up debts during their time at the tables or even if you didn’t their fate will become clear. Here in the slums of Jima one can see how the city thrives on the backs of others; entered into contracts to repay the moons they unwisely borrowed. They ultimately become slaves of the Silvermoon trade syndicate and never has their motto seemed more apt; ‘Always pay what you owe’.
