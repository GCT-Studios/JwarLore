The tiny, ember coloured fox felt them first.

He’d stopped for a quick drink in the river before heading home, and, as he lapped up the cool, forest-chilled water, a presence announced itself not far from where he crouched. Animal, mineral or vegetable, he thought jokingly. He wondered what it could be.

He was a clever fox; no ordinary little vulpine was he. The fox spent his days at the local temple, underfoot and in the way, causing as much havoc as he could before eating all the scraps and falling asleep on Master Enos’s finest robes.

The fox did not waste his time in the Temple of the Trees, and he had begun to study these monks of Ro- Kan as intently as they studied him. He clearly understood Ki, his role in the world, and possessed teeth sharp enough to lessen the stoutest of grips. He had probably mastered more in his short time there than most of the apprentice monks had learned in years. But I digress…

He felt the unmistakable pressure one feels just before a violent thunderstorm unleashes its fury on the world, a tightening across the top of his head and down his spine, all the way to the tip of his snowcapped tail. The birds went silent, and a chill entered his immediate surroundings.

A sudden buzzing noise forced the fox to flatten his ears.

The sound? High-pitched and painful. He dropped to his stomach and slid backwards as slowly and secretly as possible until deeply ensconced within the undergrowth that dotted the meandering riverbank. From here, he could look, listen, and hopefully remain unseen.

The buzzing noise gave way to what sounded like the wind blowing between the trees, yet not a leaf stirred above his orange head. His nose detected a strange and unknown scent, almost a no-smell, the absence of a smell, a smell shaped hole where something should have been. His den-uncle, who had spent time in the Empire, had described the deep desert smelling this way.

Suddenly, out the corner of his eye, he saw what appeared to be several robed creatures stepping out of a large hole floating above the forest floor. They moved quickly and quietly and seemed well-practised in this. They did not speak, except to move The Fox By Alasdair Cunningham their fingers, make strange gestures with their hands, then point and motion in the sun’s direction. The fox could not see if this pleased or displeased them, as shadow shrouded their faces from view; deep within the cowls of their dark robes, their emotions lay hidden for now. And what robes they appeared to be! Tattered and worn, but not because of carelessness or neglect, but ravaged with the passage of time… they looked ragged and of a faded colour that could have once been black or purple. The robes had the appearance of floating, like a drop of the darkest ink in a bowl of water, slowly twisting and turning, spreading ever outwards. The air shimmered and danced around them as they moved stealthily among the trees and bushes, careful not to disturb the forest litter.

At their head, a curious creation of skin, scars and helm. It wore no robes, only soft leggings and sturdy boots, with its head fully ensconced in a strange helmet with no holes allowing its wearer to see, speak, or breathe. A strange sigil anointed the helm, circular in design, as if drawn with charcoal, except this one appeared wrought in metal.

Deep scars crisscrossed the body, and the fox could see these had to be through design rather than accident, and not made with claw or sword; nay, these had to have been fashioned with darker arts, almost certainly ceremonial. It seemed like this one had made the hole they’d come from, wielding strange forces no one could understand, especially not a fox.

The view through the hole revealed a world far different from the one the fox hid within and that surrounded him. The other place appeared cold and snowing, and a scrim of frost developed rapidly in the air around the hole. It smelt different too, as if it were old, ancient even and dying, rotting from the inside out. A memory began to form quickly in the fox’s mind.

He recalled a beaten sheet of bronze one could use to see one’s reflection or reflect light throughout the darkness of the temple. The younger monks sometimes came to see what they looked like, always slightly upset as if what they found there disturbed them somewhat.

The young fox had tried it too and did not like what he saw there.

Yes, it appeared to be him but a dimmer version of what he’d seen in rivers and ponds. The creature in the metal appeared somehow darker, angrier, hateful, and wreathed in shadow as if it were a feral or rabid den-brother the fox looked at, and not itself. It possessed an ill-intentioned cunning around its eyes and a constant hunger in its grin. These robed-wanderers reminded the fox of the creatures that dwelt in the mirror; they looked and felt like a darker version of the monks he lived with, the monks from the Temple of Ro-Kan.

Whoever these strange intruders happened to be, they meant only harm, of that he could be sure. That the intruders passed quietly before him, heading deeper into the forest, away from the temple, filled the little fox with relief. He hoped it would give him time to run home and warn the others that something strange was afoot. From nothing had come...something, and that something was not natural, not a part of the natural order of things. But then, on the wind, a voice from among these strangers said, “ This is the place. We wait. Here. For the coming of the black sun.”

And with that, the fox bolted home as fast as he could, with fear in his trembling heart and yelps of doom on his lips.
