source: New Dawn rulebook
title: Temple of Ro-Kan
---
Since the start of time the Temple of Ro-Kan has stood in defence of Jwar and its people. Through
natural disasters and civil wars the Temple remains impartial and ever present in the lands of the Jwar Isles, only mobilising when there is a threat to universal balance they cannot ignore. In times of peace the Temple of Ro-Kan has always been a source of comfort and reassurance for the common people. In times when the world is in a state of imbalance they serve as champions
of the Jwar Isles defending those who are unaware that their own actions may have disturbed the finely poised equilibrium. In the past these threats to the balance have come from another place, to counter the threat the monks of the Temple of Ro-Kan constructed great barriers, using ancient and forgotten secrets they sealed off this world. For centuries these barriers have held firm and kept great evil at bay. Through man’s fallibility and apathy the barriers have weakened and this coincides with the return of the Oni and Yokai to the world not seen for an age.  

It is no coincidence that the Temple of Ro-Kan’s influence has long been fading in the Jwar Isles. Historically the spiritual guardians and defenders of the people, their strength is now waning, sapped by the rise in popularity of Michi as well as the corruption of man’s morality by the temptations of Yurei, the Silvermoon and his own base desires. All leave the Temple badly positioned to face the challenges now looming over the inhabitants of the Jwar Isles.  

The Temple is strongest in those outposts and hamlets furthest from the main population hubs where their teachings of [[Subarashī Baransu]] are still heeded and followed. It is from these last bastions of the Temple that they wait, watch and listen for signs that the time is right to strike back.  

The structure of the Temple is based around the small temples which service the needs of the local populace, the structure and appearance of these temples tend to vary but are always constructed of local materials and are simple and functional with no sign of the opulence of some Michi shrines. The peasants will often present their second child to the Temple to train as a monk of Ro-Kan, as well as feeding the monks in return for spiritual and physical protection, from rogue lords,
wild bandits and worse. The children in time become defenders of the town and teachers for future generations, so repeating the symbolic cycle of the mutually beneficial existence of the Temple.  

The presence of its temples has long been a thorn in the side of the local samurai lords and a cause of a long standing animosity between the Temple of Ro-Kan and the ruling Samurai clans, due to the fundamental differences in their beliefs and the resistance of the Temple to accept the clans' direct rule, and so continues the simmering tensions between the two powers.  

The young monks of Ro-Kan travel to the nearest monastery, where they take instruction in the ways of Subarashī Baransu and begin their journey of understanding and maintenance of the finite balance on which the world teeters; thus begins their service to its people. This training is led by Masters, individuals who have grasped the understanding of each of the six elements and his or her place in the world. The Temple teaches that nature is the greatest example of balance. Seen in
the cycles of the world, the rising and setting of the sun and the ever changing seasons being the prime examples. Young monks are often encouraged to learn from the animals around them as they are without the temptations of man and exist as one with their surroundings. When the monks understand nature, her rhythms, and the eternal world, they are considered ready for the next stage of their training; understanding one’s self.  

At this stage the monks are encouraged to explore their own emotions, feelings and in understanding attempt to gain control of them. For this they visit five great sites of importance to the Temple, each a true expression of one of the elements. Monks can spend a few hours to many years in deep contemplation of the reflection of the element in their own personalities. It is through this understanding, and more importantly the acceptance, that everyone has even the darkest characteristics within them that the monks can harness the power of the element. The five great sites are the Volcano of Mount Fuja, the “Tenshi no Namida Falls” at Lake Serenity, the “Old Grandfather” tree, “Itsiro’s Peak” and “Chikyu’s Rock” on the Plains of Ataka. The final and
hardest to find is the void, for this is everywhere and nowhere. Although these are the main pilgrimage sites and locations where the monks can commune directly with the Kami there are sites scattered across the Isles which are less well visited, but no less strong. Indeed many a monk has come across a natural glade or a raging river and felt the urge to stop his business and enter into a period of meditation.  

If a monk can achieve understanding of the six elements he or she holds the right to be called Master, taking their place to begin guiding new followers on their own paths to Nirvana. Although most complete the training and return to their childhood homes or move onwards to the great monasteries; there are some who, when confronted with the power available through mastery of the elements, turn to a darker more destructive path. The Temple does not propagate these tales for fear of planting the seed of temptation in the minds of their young protégée. But they are as much an enemy of the Temple as all others who would bring imbalance to the world through their selfish actions. To take the right path, one must know the wrong one.
